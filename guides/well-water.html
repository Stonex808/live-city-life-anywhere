Steady well water: pressure tanks and short-cycling

City water arrives at steady pressure. A well makes its own, one pump start at a time. Get the tank right and the pump lasts longer, the shower stays steady and backup power gets easier.

1. Why a pressure tank exists

A well pump is either on or off. The pressure switch turns it on when house pressure falls to the cut-in setting and off when it reaches cut-out (common pairs are 30/50 and 40/60 psi). The pressure tank holds a cushion of compressed air above a bladder or diaphragm, so water already under pressure is waiting when you open a tap. Without it, the pump would start for every glass of water.

The water a tank delivers between cut-out and cut-in is its drawdown. That, not the tank’s gallon size, is what protects the pump.

2. Short-cycling: the symptom to fix

Short-cycling means the pump starts and stops every few seconds. Each start pulls a surge of current and heats the motor and the switch contacts, so frequent starts wear both out.

Signs

  • The pressure switch clicks on and off rapidly while one tap is open.
  • The pressure gauge swings quickly from cut-out to cut-in.
  • Lights dim or flicker each time the pump starts.
  • Pressure pulses in the shower.

Usual causes

  • Waterlogged tank: it lost its air charge or the bladder failed.
  • Wrong precharge for the switch setting.
  • Undersized tank for the pump’s flow.
  • A leak or a stuck fixture that keeps drawing water.

3. The 1-minute rule

Rule of thumb

Size the tank so the pump runs at least 1 minute each cycle: drawdown needed (gal) = pump GPM × 1. On larger pumps, 2 minutes is the safer target rule of thumb. If your pump maker gives a minimum run time, use theirs.

Drawdown is much smaller than the tank. On the reference table the Tank Sizer publishes (Amtrol Well-X-Trol specifications card MC7025), a 20-gallon WX-202 gives about 6.2 gallons of drawdown at 30/50 psi, and only about 4.7 gallons at 50/70. Higher pressure settings mean less drawdown from the same tank.

Worked example

Pump: 10 GPM · Switch: 30/50 · Target: 1 minute Drawdown needed = 10 × 1 = 10 gal On the sizer's Amtrol table at 30/50: WX-203 (32 gal) ≈ 9.9 gal → just short WX-205 (34 gal) ≈ 10.5 gal → smallest that meets 10 gal Precharge (tank empty) = 30 − 2 = 28 psi

Other brands publish their own drawdown charts. Always check the chart for the exact tank you buy.

Quick check: minimum drawdown and precharge

Without JavaScript: drawdown needed = GPM × minutes. Precharge = cut-in − 2 psi, set with the tank empty.

4. Find your pump’s GPM (nameplate wins)

  1. Paperwork first. The well driller’s log or pump installer’s invoice usually lists pump model, horsepower and rated GPM. The pump model is also often on the control box.
  2. Look up the model. The maker’s spec sheet gives rated GPM. A pump’s actual flow drops as pressure and lift go up, so treat rated GPM as the high side.
  3. No paperwork? Use the run-time test below. It answers the real question (how long does the pump run per cycle?) without knowing GPM at all.
Don’t guess from horsepower

Horsepower tells you power, not flow. Two ½ HP pumps can deliver very different GPM depending on well depth and pump design. If you must estimate, label it an estimate and size up.

5. Two 5-minute tests anyone can do

A. Pump run-time test (are you short-cycling?)

  1. Make sure nothing else is using water.
  2. Open a tap until the pump turns on (the switch clicks, the gauge starts climbing). Close the tap.
  3. Time how long the pump runs until it shuts off at cut-out.
  4. Under about a minute with no water running? The tank isn’t storing enough. Check precharge first (below), then tank size.

B. Drawdown test (what your tank really delivers)

  1. Wait for the pump to shut off at cut-out.
  2. Run water into a bucket marked in gallons until the pump turns back on.
  3. The gallons collected are your real drawdown. Compare to GPM × 1 minute.

6. Set the precharge

Rule

Set the empty-tank air charge to about 2 psi below cut-in: 28 psi for a 30/50 switch, 38 psi for 40/60.

  1. Turn off the pump breaker.
  2. Open a tap until water stops flowing, so the tank is empty of water.
  3. Check the air valve on top of the tank with an ordinary tire gauge. Add air with a pump or compressor, or let some out, to reach cut-in − 2.
  4. Close the tap, turn the breaker back on, and watch one full cycle.
Water from the air valve = failed bladder

If pressing the valve stem spits water, the bladder or diaphragm has failed. Adding air won’t fix it; the tank needs replacing. Also stop and call a well contractor if the tank is rusted through, bulging, or the pressure relief valve is releasing.

Constant-pressure (variable-speed) systems often use smaller tanks and their own precharge rules. Follow the drive manufacturer’s manual, not the cut-in − 2 rule.

7. Water when the power is out

With no power, the pump can’t run, and the tank gives you roughly one drawdown (a few gallons) and then stops. Plan around that:

  • Store drinking water. Ready.gov’s planning figure is one gallon per person per day, for several days.
  • When an outage is forecast, fill a bathtub or buckets for flushing toilets (pour into the bowl to flush).
  • To run the pump on backup power, size for its surge and 240 V. See Backup power.
  • When power returns after a long outage, expect air and sediment in the lines. Run an outside tap first.
