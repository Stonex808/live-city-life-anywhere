Guide 03 · Warm rooms, fewer surprises

Heat without the grid: space heaters, fuel heat and circuits

City apartments come with heat you never think about. Out here you choose: oil, propane, gas, a heat pump, wood, or a plug-in heater for one room. Here is how to choose without tripping breakers or wasting money.

1. Which question are you asking?

“Can I heat one room cheaper?”

That’s a cost question. A plug-in heater only saves money if it lets you turn the whole-house thermostat down. Jump to cost.

“How do I stay warm when the power is out?”

That’s a backup question. Most central heat (even gas, propane and oil) needs electricity for a blower, burner or pump. Jump to outage heat.

3. Circuit math: the 12 A and 16 A lines

Amps = watts ÷ volts. A 1,500 W heater on a 120 V outlet draws 12.5 A.

The 80% guideline

The National Electrical Code treats loads that run for hours as continuous and keeps them to 80% of the circuit rating. A heater cycling on its thermostat all evening counts. That gives:

15 A circuit → 12 A (1,440 W)  ·  20 A circuit → 16 A (1,920 W)

On one 15 A circuitAmps at 120 VVerdict
One heater on 750 W6.3 AComfortable headroom
One heater on 1,500 W12.5 ASlightly over 12 A. Heaters are made for these outlets, but there is no room for anything else on that circuit.
1,500 W heater + TV (100 W) + laptop (65 W)13.9 AOver the guideline; nuisance trips likely
Two 1,500 W heaters25 AWill trip

TV and laptop watts are typical values estimate. Read the label on yours.

Find out what shares a breaker

  1. Plug a lamp or radio into the outlet you want to use.
  2. Flip breakers off one at a time until it goes dark. Note the breaker size printed on its handle (15 or 20).
  3. Walk the house with the breaker off and note every outlet and light that’s dead. All of those share the circuit.

Quick check: will this circuit hold?

Without JavaScript: amps = watts ÷ volts. Keep continuous loads at or under 12 A (1,440 W) on 15 A, 16 A (1,920 W) on 20 A.

4. Cords and clearances

Plug heaters straight into the wall

The U.S. CPSC says to plug a space heater directly into a wall outlet: no extension cords, no power strips, and never daisy-chain one strip or cord into another. A warm plug or outlet means a loose or worn connection. Stop using it.

  • Keep heaters at least 3 feet from beds, curtains, paper and anything that can burn (CPSC).
  • Choose heaters with tip-over and overheat shutoff, listed by a recognized lab (UL, ETL or CSA).
  • Turn heaters off when you leave the room or go to sleep.
  • Low settings (600, 750, 900 W) leave room on a shared circuit and are gentler on a generator.

5. Comparing cost honestly

Fair comparisons use the same unit: dollars per million BTU of heat actually delivered. You can do it with your own bills:

Electric heater $/MMBtu = ($ per kWh ÷ 3,412) × 1,000,000 Heat pump $/MMBtu = electric figure ÷ COP Oil $/MMBtu = ($ per gal ÷ 138,500) × 1,000,000 ÷ efficiency Propane $/MMBtu = ($ per gal ÷ 91,452) × 1,000,000 ÷ efficiency Natural gas $/MMBtu = ($ per therm ÷ 100,000) × 1,000,000 ÷ efficiency (heat contents: EIA; efficiency as a decimal, e.g. 0.85)

The real saving is in the setback

Per unit of heat, a plug-in heater often loses to gas, propane or a heat pump. It pays off when you heat one room and turn the rest of the house down. The U.S. Department of Energy says setting the thermostat back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day can save up to about 10% a year on heating.

6. Heat during an outage

  1. Keep your central heat running on backup power if you can. A furnace blower is a much smaller load than electric heat. The generator planner includes a gas furnace blower (planning figure 800 W running, 2,350 W starting estimate; check your furnace label).
  2. Don’t plan on 1,500 W heaters from a small generator or battery. One heater on high is 1,500 W of continuous load, often more than the pump and fridge combined. Use the 750 W setting or a fuel-based heat source if you must.
  3. Pick one warm room. Close doors, hang a blanket over openings, use the room with the fewest windows.
  4. Keep CO alarms working. Any fuel-burning heater, stove or generator nearby makes this non-negotiable.
