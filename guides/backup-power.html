Backup power for a home on a well

On city power you never think about the pump. On a well, no power means no water. This guide shows how to size backup power around the one number that decides it: the pump’s start-up surge.

1. The number that decides it: start-up surge

An electric motor pulls far more power for the first second or so than it does while running. A refrigerator compressor, a furnace blower and above all a submersible well pump all do this. Your generator or inverter has to cover that spike on top of everything already running, or it stalls, trips, or the pump hums without starting.

Rule of thumb

A well pump’s starting draw is often 2–3× its running draw estimate. Your nameplate wins over any chart, including ours.

The second thing that surprises people: most submersible well pumps run on 240 V. Many small generators and most portable power stations only provide 120 V. If the pump is on a two-pole breaker (two handles tied together), it is a 240 V load.

2. Generator or portable power station?

They solve different problems. Many off-grid and rural homes end up with both: a battery for quiet, indoor-safe essentials and a generator for the pump and multi-day outages.

Carbon monoxide kills quietly

Run a fuel generator outside only. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission advises at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away from doors, windows and vents. Put battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.

Brand note: battery brands like EcoFlow publish both continuous and surge (“peak”) watts. Compare both against your numbers. We are not paid for brand mentions.

3. Read the nameplate, not the internet

Every number you need is printed somewhere in your house. Write these down before you shop:

  1. Well pump. A submersible pump’s own label is down the well, so look at the control box (3-wire pumps have one, usually near the pressure tank), your well installer’s paperwork, or the pump breaker. Note volts, horsepower, and amps. “230 V” on a plate means a 240 V circuit.
  2. Refrigerator and freezer. The label inside the door or on the back lists volts and amps.
  3. Furnace or boiler. The blower or burner needs electricity even when the fuel is gas, propane or oil. The furnace label lists volts and amps.
  4. Look for LRA. Some motor plates list LRA (locked-rotor amps). Volts × LRA is a conservative upper bound for the start-up spike. Soft-start or variable-speed (constant-pressure) controllers cut the surge a lot.

4. The sizing math the planner uses

The Well Pump Generator Planner does this for you. Here is exactly what it calculates, so you can check any quote or listing:

Running total = sum of running watts for everything on at once Staggered peak = running total + the single largest surge (starting − running watts) Look for running rating ≥ running total ÷ 0.8 surge/starting rating ≥ staggered peak
Why divide by 0.8?

A generator that runs for hours near 100% runs hot and has no headroom. Keeping continuous load at or under about 80% of the running rating is a standard planning margin.

The planner starts with a ½ HP well pump, refrigerator, gas furnace blower, ten LED lights and Wi-Fi. These are its typical planning figures estimates; replace them with your nameplate numbers.

Notice the gap: starting everything at once needs about 6,800 W of surge, while starting one motor at a time needs about 4,200 W. That is the difference between a large generator and a mid-size one.

5. Stagger your starts

Motor surges last about a second. If they never overlap, a smaller generator can carry the same house. Tape this order inside your panel door:

  1. Before connecting: switch the big loads off (pump breaker, furnace switch, fridge unplugged or its breaker off).
  2. Start the generator outdoors and let it settle for a minute.
  3. Turn on the load with the biggest surge first. Wait about 10 seconds.
  4. Turn on the next biggest. Wait. Repeat for each motor.
  5. Small loads (lights, Wi-Fi, chargers) last.

The planner prints this start order for your exact loads.

6. Connecting to the house safely

Never backfeed through a dryer outlet

A “double male” cord plugged into a dryer or range outlet can send generator power back out onto the utility lines, where it can kill a lineworker, and it leaves live, exposed prongs on the cord. It also bypasses your panel’s protection. Don’t do it, even briefly.

  • A 240 V well pump needs a proper connection: a transfer switch or a panel interlock kit with a generator inlet. Choose and install it with a licensed electrician so it matches your panel. Permits are required in many areas.
  • Match the cord to the generator outlet and inlet (many 240 V setups use a 30 A, 4-prong twist-lock cord). Don’t use a lighter cord than the outlet is rated for.
  • Refuel only with the engine off and cool. Store fuel in approved containers away from the house.
  • Run the generator under load periodically so you find problems before the outage. Monthly is a common habit.

7. How long will a battery last?

Battery stations are rated in watt-hours (Wh). A rough runtime estimate:

hours ≈ capacity (Wh) × 0.85 ÷ average load (W) (0.85 = inverter losses, an estimate)

Example arithmetic: a 1,000 Wh station feeding a steady 150 W load gives about 1,000 × 0.85 ÷ 150 ≈ 5.7 hours. A fridge cycles on and off, so its average draw is far below its running watts. Measure it with a plug-in watt meter over 24 hours instead of guessing.
