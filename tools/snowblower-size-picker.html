Your driveway and your snow

One car ≈ 10–12 ft wide, two cars ≈ 20–24 ft. Length × width is used when both are filled in; otherwise the total area is used.

Walking pace is an estimate: ~2 mph is about 176 ft a minute, ~1.5 mph 132, ~1 mph 88. It varies with the snow, the machine and you.

Width by width: does 28 beat 24 for you?

Same driveway, same pace, different deck widths. This is where the 24-vs-28 argument usually settles: on a short two-car drive the wider deck saves a minute or so; on a long or wide drive the minutes add up every storm.

Planning tool, not a rating

These are planning estimates from common buying-guide rules of thumb. They don’t replace the manufacturer’s rating for a specific model (intake height, clearing width, recommended surfaces) or your local snow conditions. Read the owner’s manual before using any snowblower.

How the math works

1. Which class

These are rules of thumb that retailer and buying guides (Consumer Reports, home-center and manufacturer buying guides) commonly repeat. They are planning estimates, not specs for any model.

ClassUsually suggested forAvoid when
Single-stagePaved drives and walks, lighter snow, storms up to about 8 in rule of thumb. One auger picks up and throws the snow.Gravel or dirt: the auger touches the surface and can throw stones. Also deep or heavy wet snow and steep slopes.
Compact two-stageSmaller drives that still get heavy snow, have gravel or slope. Usually 21–24 in.Long drives or very deep storms, where a full-size deck saves time.
Two-stageDeeper snow, heavy or wet snow, gravel and unpaved drives, slopes, plow piles. An auger feeds an impeller that throws the snow. Skid shoes keep the housing slightly above the surface.Tiny paved areas with light snow (more machine than you need).
Three-stageMarketed for big drives with deep, heavy or icy snow. An extra front accelerator feeds the auger.Light-snow areas. A two-stage of the same width does most of the same work.

The picker suggests two-stage if any of these are true: gravel or unpaved surface, typical storms over 8 in, heavy or wet snow, a slope, a long throw, a plow pile, or more than about 2,000 sq ft to clear. Otherwise it suggests single-stage. If storms are deeper than 12 in, the snow is heavy or there’s a plow pile, and the drive is over 1,200 sq ft, it lists three-stage as an option.

2. Which width

Wider decks mean fewer passes, but they also mean a heavier, bigger machine. These area bands are our planning estimates, loosely following how buying guides group 1-car, 2-car and long drives:

Single-stage: ≤ 500 sq ft → 18–21 in · otherwise 21–22 in Compact 2-stg: ≤ 800 sq ft → 21–24 in Two-stage: ≤ 1,200 sq ft → 24 in ≤ 2,500 sq ft → 26–28 in ≤ 4,500 sq ft → 30 in larger → 30 in or wider

3. Passes and time

effective cut = clearing width × 0.85 (≈15% overlap per pass, estimate) passes = driveway width ÷ effective cut, rounded up walking = passes × driveway length (+ extra area ÷ effective cut) time = walking ÷ pace + ~10 s per turnaround pace ≈ 176 ft/min at ~2 mph (estimate; deep or wet snow is slower)

If you only enter an area, the picker treats it as a 12 ft wide strip of the same area to count passes. That’s close enough for time, but enter length and width for an accurate pass count.

Worked example: the classic 24 vs 28 question

Two-car drive 50 × 20 ft, paved 24 in: cut 1.70 ft → 20 ÷ 1.70 = 11.8 → 12 passes · 600 ft · ~5 min 28 in: cut 1.98 ft → 20 ÷ 1.98 = 10.1 → 11 passes · 550 ft · ~5 min Long single drive 200 × 12 ft, gravel 24 in: 12 ÷ 1.70 = 7.1 → 8 passes · 1,600 ft · ~10 min 28 in: 12 ÷ 1.98 = 6.1 → 7 passes · 1,400 ft · ~9 min 30 in: 12 ÷ 2.13 = 5.6 → 6 passes · 1,200 ft · ~8 min

On a 50 ft two-car drive, the width barely changes the time. Pick for the snow and the surface first, then for storage space and weight. Width starts to pay on long or wide drives, and when the driveway width divides neatly by the cut.

Single vs two-stage in one line

Gravel, deep storms, heavy snow, hills or a plow pile → two-stage. Paved, light to average snow, typically under ~8 in → single-stage is enough. rule of thumb

Safety

Never clear a clogged chute or auger with your hands, even with the engine off. Stop the engine, wait for moving parts to stop, and use the clean-out tool. Run gas engines outdoors only. Keep children and pets away while you clear. These are the standard U.S. CPSC and owner’s-manual warnings.
