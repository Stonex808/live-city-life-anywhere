Math (estimates): a shower uses flow × minutes, and about 78% of that is tank water at 120°F mixed down to 105°F with 50°F winter supply water. About 70% of a tank comes out hot before it runs lukewarm (plumber rule of thumb). Plus 2 gal of hot water per person per day for sinks. Gas tank with a plug: the blower runs only while the burner fires; we use ~200 W while firing (a common replacement blower is rated 1.8 A at 115 V) plus ~5 W standby, and a 40,000 BTU/h burner at 80%. Gas tankless: ~100 W while water flows plus ~5 W standby; outdoor freeze protection adds ~100 W for 12 cold hours a day. Battery: 85% of rated Wh reaches the outlet. Planning numbers, not tests of any heater.

Read before you plug anything in

  • Hardwired heater? Don't improvise a cord. Only heaters that already plug into an outlet can go on a UPS or power station. Anything else needs an electrician.
  • Electric tank: shut the breaker off if the tank might get drained. Power back on with an empty tank burns out the elements.
  • Outdoor tankless in a freeze with no power: most makers say drain it. Check your manual before the storm.
  • Any gas appliance: keep a battery CO alarm working. Headache, dizziness or nausea: get out and call 911.

Stretch the hot water

  • Use the hot water early. A full tank cools slowly, but it never reheats while the power is out (unless it's a pilot-flame gas tank).
  • Short showers, low-flow head. Every minute you cut is about 1.5–2 gal of hot water saved.
  • Your tank is also stored drinking water if the taps stop. Shut the cold inlet valve first so it stays clean.
  • Power vent: buy pure sine. A cheap modified-sine UPS may not light the burner. Test it before an outage.

Rheem and A.O. Smith list residential power-vent tanks at up to about 5 A at 120 V; that's the maximum, so we size the backup to your label amps × 120 V. Gas tankless units list their own amps on the rating plate. Electric tanks usually use 4,500 W elements on 240 V: a power station can't run one, and a generator needs a 240 V outlet plus an electrician-installed transfer switch or interlock. Watts while running are not published by most makers; measure yours with a plug-in watt meter. Planning information only.
